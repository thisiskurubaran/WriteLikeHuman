# Recommendation and reference letters

Help a teacher, manager, or mentor write a recommendation or reference letter in their own words, based on what they actually saw the person do.

## When this applies

- A professor, teacher, manager, or client writes a recommendation for a student, employee, or freelancer.
- A reference letter for a job, a rental, a visa, a scholarship, or a university application.
- A student or employee drafts a letter for their recommender to review and sign. This happens often, so handle it honestly (see below).

## What stays the same

- Names, roles, course names, company names, dates, grades, and how long the recommender has known the person, exactly as given.
- The recommender's real opinion and level of enthusiasm. A good, honest letter beats an inflated one.
- Only what the recommender observed. Achievements, projects, and qualities come from them.

## What you never do

- Invent achievements, awards, rankings ("top 1 percent of students I've taught"), projects, or skills.
- Exaggerate. If the recommender says the student was "a solid, reliable member of the lab", don't turn it into "the most brilliant researcher I have ever met".
- Write a letter for the recommender to sign without them having seen and agreed to every claim.

## When the person being recommended wrote the draft

Many recommenders ask the student or employee to draft the letter. Help them, and be clear about it:

- Keep every claim to things the recommender actually witnessed or can check.
- Write it in the recommender's voice and from their point of view, but flag that the recommender must read it, change it, and agree with every sentence before signing.
- If the user asks you to add things the recommender didn't see or wouldn't confirm, decline that part and explain briefly.

## Steps

1. Find out who is writing, who it's for, what it's for (job, course, visa, rental), and how long and in what role they've known the person.
2. Ask for two or three specific things the recommender saw: a project, a moment, a result, a habit. Specific beats general.
3. Open with the relationship and the recommendation in one or two sentences.
4. Give the specific examples, each with what the person did and why it mattered.
5. Close with a clear, honest level of support and an offer to be contacted, if the recommender wants that.
6. Use the format the user needs: a letter with a date and signature block, or a short form answer for an online portal.
7. Check every name, date, and figure against what the user gave you.

## Output

The letter, ready for the recommender to review, followed by a short change summary. If the user is the person being recommended, add one line reminding them the recommender needs to read and agree with every claim before signing.

## Example

Notes from the recommender, Dr. Meera Nair: "Taught Kavya in Data Structures (2025) and supervised her final-year project on bus arrival predictions for Kochi. She cleaned two years of messy GPS data herself and explained her results clearly to the transport office."

Letter body:

> I taught Kavya Menon in Data Structures in 2025 and supervised her final-year project, so I've seen her work closely for over a year.
>
> For her project, Kavya built a model to predict bus arrival times in Kochi. The hardest part was the data: two years of GPS records with gaps and errors. She cleaned it herself, carefully and without being asked to do it a particular way. When she presented her results to the city transport office, she explained them in plain terms that non-specialists could follow, which is rarer than it should be.
>
> I recommend Kavya for your master's programme without hesitation. I'm happy to answer any questions.

What I changed
- Built the letter around the two things you actually saw: the GPS data cleaning and the presentation to the transport office.
- Kept the course name, year, and project topic exactly as you gave them.
- Avoided ranking claims you didn't make.
