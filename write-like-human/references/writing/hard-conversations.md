# Apologies and hard conversations

Help people say difficult things to friends and family in their own words: saying sorry, setting a boundary, or turning down a request.

## When this applies

- Apologising to a friend, partner, sibling, or parent after a fight or a mistake.
- Setting a boundary: "please call before visiting", "I won't discuss my salary", "I need a weekend to myself".
- Turning down family requests: lending money, hosting relatives for a month, attending every function, pressure to get married or have children.
- The user has a draft that sounds cold, over-apologetic, or like a corporate statement.

For work apologies by email, use `writing/email.md`. For chat-style replies, also load `writing/chat-replies.md`.

## What stays the same

- What the user wants to say. If they want to say no, the message says no. Do not soften it into maybe.
- What they are sorry for, and what they are not sorry for. Do not add apologies they did not offer.
- Facts about what happened, in their words.
- Names, relationships, and how they address the person (Amma, bhaiya, didi, uncle).
- The language and level of formality they use with this person.

## What to avoid

- Manipulation. No guilt-tripping, no "after everything I've done for you", no veiled threats, no emotional pressure dressed up as care. Help the user be clear, not to win.
- Fake sweetness. Piling on "dear" and "love you so much" before a no reads as insincere.
- Over-explaining. A long list of reasons invites argument. One honest reason is enough, or none.
- Therapy speak the user would never say: "I'm holding space for your feelings", "this doesn't serve me".
- Non-apologies: "I'm sorry if you felt hurt". Say what they did and that they are sorry for it.

## Steps

1. Work out the one thing the message must get across: sorry, no, or please stop.
2. Say it early. Do not bury it after three paragraphs of warm-up.
3. For an apology: name what they did, say sorry once, say what they will do differently if they told you. Do not ask for forgiveness on a deadline.
4. For a boundary: say what they need in plain words, and what they will do if it is not respected, only if they told you.
5. For a no: say no clearly, give one reason if the user wants to, and offer an alternative only if they mentioned one.
6. Keep the warmth that is real. If the user loves the person, one sincere line can say so.
7. Keep it short. Hard messages land better in a few lines.
8. Read it once as the other person would. Remove anything that sounds like a lecture.

## Safety

If the user describes abuse, threats, violence, or feeling unsafe, gently say that their safety comes first and that they do not have to handle it with a message alone. Suggest talking to someone they trust or a local helpline. In India, the women's helpline is 181 and the emergency number is 112. Then help with the message if they still want it. Do not push.

## Output

The message, ready to send. Then a short change summary. If the user seems unsure about the tone, offer one line: "Want it a bit softer, or more direct?"

## Example

Before (to an uncle asking for a loan):

> Dear Uncle, I hope you are doing well. I am extremely sorry but unfortunately due to various financial commitments and unforeseen expenses I regret to inform you that I may not be in a position to extend the loan at this point in time. I hope you understand and I am truly sorry for any disappointment.

After:

> Hi Uncle, I thought about it, but I can't lend the money right now. We're still paying off the flat and I don't have the room. I'm sorry I can't help with this one.

What I changed
- Put the no in the first line instead of the middle.
- Swapped "various financial commitments" for the flat loan you mentioned.
- Cut "may not be in a position", which sounded like a maybe.
- Apologised once instead of three times.
