# Cross-cultural check

Point out where a message might land badly with a reader from another country or culture: too blunt, too familiar, too roundabout, or simply confusing. This is a report. Rewrite only if the user asks.

## When this applies

- "Will this sound okay to my client in Germany?", "is this too direct for a Japanese reader?", "I'm emailing someone in the US for the first time, check this."
- The user mentions the reader is in another country, or the message goes to an international team.
- Indian English writers sending to readers abroad, and readers abroad writing to someone in India.

## What stays the same

- The message itself, until the user asks for changes.
- The user's meaning and intent. If they want to say no, the advice helps them say no clearly in a way the reader will take well, not turn it into a maybe.
- Names, numbers, dates, and amounts.

## How to talk about it

Talk about what the reader is likely to expect in that setting, not about national character. Say "In many German workplaces, people expect the request in the first line" rather than "Germans are direct." People vary. Present every note as a likely reading, not a rule, and never as a stereotype. Never comment on religion, ethnicity, or appearance.

## What to look for

- Too blunt for the setting: a request with no greeting or thanks where the reader likely expects some warmth first.
- Too indirect for the setting: the actual request buried after three paragraphs, or a "no" so soft it reads as "yes".
- Too familiar too soon: first names, "dear", "bro", emoji, or jokes with someone the user has never met, where the reader's workplace is likely more formal.
- Too formal for the setting: "Respected Sir" or "Kindly do the needful" to a reader who expects plain first-name emails.
- Idioms that don't travel: "touch base", "ballpark figure", "it's not rocket science", cricket or baseball phrases, local slang.
- Indian English phrases that confuse readers abroad: "do the needful", "revert" for reply, "prepone", "kindly adjust", "out of station", "pass out" for graduate, "cousin brother". Fine inside India; worth flagging for an international reader. Never flag them for an Indian reader.
- Date and number formats: "03/04" means 3 April in India and the UK but 4 March in the US. "2 lakh" means nothing to most readers outside South Asia. Suggest writing the month as a word and giving the figure in the reader's style too, if the user wants.
- Time zones: "call at 5" with no time zone, for a reader in another country.
- Humour and sarcasm, which often read as rude in writing to someone who doesn't know the writer.

## Steps

1. Ask, in one line, where the reader is and how well the user knows them, if it isn't clear. Skip the question if the message already makes it obvious.
2. Read the message as that reader likely would.
3. List only the notes that would change how the message lands. Three to six is plenty.
4. For each, quote the bit, say how it might read, and give a short alternative.
5. Offer to rewrite. If the user says yes, apply the notes and follow rewrite mode for everything else.

## Output

```
For a first email to a client in the Netherlands

- "Kindly do the needful": may confuse them. Try "Could you sign and return the form?"
- The request is in the last paragraph. They'll likely expect it near the top.
- "Revert by EOD": "revert" may read as "undo". Try "reply by 5 pm your time (Amsterdam)".
- "03/04": could be read as 3 April or 4 March. Write "3 April".

Want me to rewrite it with these changes?
```

## Example

Before (to a new contact in the US):

> Respected Sir, greetings of the day. With reference to our telephonic conversation, kindly revert with the quotation at the earliest. Please do the needful.

Notes:

```
- "Respected Sir, greetings of the day" reads very formal in a US
  business email. "Hi Mr. Collins," is enough.
- "Revert" may read as "undo". Use "reply" or "send".
- "Do the needful" isn't used in the US and may confuse.
- "At the earliest" works, but a date is clearer: "by Friday".
```

After (once the user asked for a rewrite):

> Hi Mr. Collins, thanks for the call yesterday. Could you send the quotation by Friday? Thanks, Anil

(Made up names. "By Friday" and "yesterday" would come from the user; if they didn't give a date, keep "as soon as you can".)
