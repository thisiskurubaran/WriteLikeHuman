# Tone presets

Change how something sounds without changing what it says. Five presets cover most requests.

## When this applies

- The user uses a tone word: friendlier, warmer, more professional, more formal, shorter, more direct, less stiff, less rude, more polite.
- They say who it is for and the current tone clearly does not fit ("this is for my boss" on a very casual draft).

If they do not ask, keep the tone the text already has. Offer a change in one line at the end only if it would clearly help.

## What stays the same

- The facts, the request, and the decision. A polite no stays a no.
- Names, dates, numbers, amounts, and commitments.
- Length within about 10 percent, except for "short and direct", which is meant to cut.
- The language and dialect. Indian English stays Indian English.

## The presets

### Friendly

Sounds like a colleague or friend who likes you.

- Use contractions (it's, we'll, don't).
- Use the person's first name.
- Shorter sentences. A light, natural opener ("Hope your week's going okay") only if the text has room.
- Do not add jokes, slang, or emoji the user did not use.

### Professional

Clear and courteous. The default for work email.

- Lead with the point.
- Plain words over formal ones ("start" not "commence").
- Keep pleasantries to one line or none.
- Contractions are fine in most workplaces. Drop them if the original avoids them.

### Warm

Kind and personal. For thank you notes, support, and personal messages.

- Say what the person did and why it mattered.
- Use "I" and "you" more than "we" and "one".
- Keep it sincere. One specific line beats three general ones.
- No gushing ("I am beyond grateful for your incredible generosity").

### Short and direct

For busy readers and quick replies.

- Lead with the answer or the ask.
- Cut greetings to a word or two, or none in chat.
- One idea per sentence. Bullets for more than two items.
- Stay polite. Direct is not rude. Keep "please" and "thanks".

### Formal

For government, bank, legal, and official letters, and senior people the user does not know.

- Full sentences, no contractions, no slang.
- Respectful salutations and closings (see `writing/formal-letters.md` for Indian formats).
- Still plain. Formal does not mean "I humbly beg to state that". Use modern formal English unless the user's office expects the older style.

## Steps

1. Pick the preset that matches the user's word. "Nicer" is usually friendly or warm. "Less rude" is usually professional.
2. Change only what the preset changes. Leave structure alone unless it fights the tone.
3. Check you did not overshoot: a friendly version should still be appropriate for the reader.
4. Mention the tone change in the change summary in plain words.

## Example

Before (short and blunt):

> Report is late again. Need it by 5 today.

Professional:

> Hi Deepak, the report hasn't come in yet. Could you send it by 5 pm today? Thanks.

Friendly:

> Hey Deepak, just checking on the report. Any chance you can get it to me by 5 today? Thanks!
