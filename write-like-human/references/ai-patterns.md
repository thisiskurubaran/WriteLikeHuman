# AI writing patterns

These are the habits that make writing read like a chatbot wrote it. Read this file on every request. Use it two ways:

- When rewriting or drafting, avoid these patterns.
- When checking, look for them and explain them to the user in plain words.

One pattern on its own is not proof of anything. People use dashes and the word "crucial" too. What gives text away is several of these stacked together. Fix the stack, not every single instance. Never tell the user which numbered pattern you found; describe it in everyday words.

This file names some of the words it warns about. That is on purpose.

## Words and phrases

### 1. Stock AI words

Why it reads as AI: these words show up far more in chatbot output than in normal writing. A sentence with two of them sounds generated.

Watch for: delve, leverage, robust, seamless, elevate, unlock, empower, game-changer, cutting-edge, comprehensive, crucial, pivotal, tapestry, realm, testament, landscape (used for anything that is not land), navigate (used for anything that is not a route), foster, harness, embark, bustling, vibrant, meticulous, intricate, showcase, streamline, holistic, synergy, paramount, myriad, plethora, underscore, boasts.

- Before: "This guide will help you navigate the complex landscape of home loans."
- After: "This guide explains how home loans work."

### 2. Inflated importance

Why it reads as AI: ordinary things get described as historic, transformative, or vital. People rarely talk about a team lunch as "a pivotal moment".

- Before: "The new canteen menu marks a significant milestone in our journey toward employee wellbeing."
- After: "The canteen has a new menu with more vegetarian options."

### 3. Sales language

Why it reads as AI: everything is "exciting", "powerful", "world-class", or "the perfect solution". It sounds like an ad, even in a leave letter.

- Before: "I'm thrilled to share this exciting opportunity to elevate your morning routine."
- After: "Here's a coffee maker I've been using for a month."

### 4. Hedging stacks

Why it reads as AI: several soft words piled on one claim. "It could potentially be argued that it may perhaps help."

- Before: "This could potentially help to somewhat improve results in some cases."
- After: "This may improve results."

### 5. Filler phrases

Why it reads as AI: words that take space and say nothing. "It's important to note that", "It's worth mentioning", "In order to", "At the end of the day", "When it comes to".

- Before: "It's important to note that the office will be closed on Monday."
- After: "The office is closed on Monday."

### 6. Fancy words where plain ones work

Why it reads as AI: "utilize" for use, "commence" for start, "endeavor" for try, "facilitate" for help, "ascertain" for find out.

- Before: "We will commence the meeting once all participants have arrived."
- After: "We'll start once everyone is here."

### 7. Vague credit

Why it reads as AI: claims pinned on no one. "Experts say", "Studies show", "Many believe", with no expert or study named.

- Before: "Experts agree that walking after dinner is beneficial."
- After: name the source if the user gave one. If not, keep the claim modest: "Walking after dinner helps some people with digestion." Never invent a source.

### 8. Stand-in verbs for "is"

Why it reads as AI: chatbots avoid the plain word "is". Things "serve as", "stand as", "act as", or "function as" something, even when nothing is serving anyone.

- Before: "The community hall serves as a gathering place for residents."
- After: "Residents meet in the community hall."

### 9. Signpost starters

Why it reads as AI: sentences open with a label that announces what kind of sentence is coming. "In essence", "Simply put", "That said", "Notably", "Importantly", "It's worth noting". Two or three in one short text is a strong tell.

- Before: "Simply put, the rent is going up. That said, parking is still free."
- After: "The rent is going up, but parking is still free."

## Sentence shapes

### 10. "Not just X, but Y"

Why it reads as AI: chatbots love setting up a contrast nobody asked for. "It's not just a phone, it's a lifestyle." Also "It's not about X. It's about Y."

- Before: "This isn't just a birthday party, it's a celebration of friendship."
- After: "We're having a birthday party, and all of Riya's old friends are coming."

### 11. Forced groups of three

Why it reads as AI: lists of exactly three, again and again, often with the third item padded. "Fast, reliable, and efficient." "Learn, grow, and thrive."

- Before: "Our team is dedicated, passionate, and committed to excellence."
- After: "Our team cares about getting it right." Or keep two items if two are real.

### 12. Rhetorical question setups

Why it reads as AI: a question asked only to answer it straight away. "So what does this mean for you? It means..."

- Before: "Why does this matter? Because deadlines affect everyone."
- After: "Deadlines affect everyone, so this matters."

### 13. "-ing" tails that add fake depth

Why it reads as AI: a sentence ends with a comma and a clause like "highlighting the importance of...", "reflecting a commitment to...", "ensuring that...".

- Before: "The school added two buses, reflecting its commitment to student safety."
- After: "The school added two buses so fewer kids walk on the main road."

### 14. Same length, same rhythm

Why it reads as AI: every sentence is 15 to 20 words and built the same way. Real writing mixes short and long.

- Before: "The project started in March. The team met every week. The results were shared in June. The client was happy with the work."
- After: "We started in March and met every week. By June we had results. The client was happy."

### 15. Overused connectors

Why it reads as AI: "Moreover", "Furthermore", "Additionally", "In addition", "Consequently" at the start of sentence after sentence.

- Before: "Furthermore, the rent includes parking. Moreover, the society has a gym."
- After: "Rent includes parking, and the society has a gym."

### 16. False ranges

Why it reads as AI: "from X to Y" where X and Y are not ends of any real scale. "From small startups to global brands", "from breakfast to big decisions".

- Before: "Our app helps with everything from grocery lists to life goals."
- After: "Our app keeps your lists in one place."

### 17. Passive voice that hides who did it

Why it reads as AI: "Mistakes were made", "It was decided that", when the writer knows who did it.

- Before: "It has been decided that the trip will be postponed."
- After: "We've decided to postpone the trip." (Only if the user said who decided. Otherwise leave it.)

### 18. "Here's the thing" setups

Why it reads as AI: a little drumroll before an ordinary point. "Here's the thing:", "Here's why that matters:", "The truth is", "Let's be honest", "The reality is". It promises a reveal and then says something plain.

- Before: "Here's the thing: most people don't read long emails."
- After: "Most people don't read long emails."

### 19. "Whether it's X, Y, or Z" framing

Why it reads as AI: a list of three examples wrapped in "Whether it's..." to sound complete, often in the middle of a text, not just the opening. The list usually adds nothing.

- Before: "Whether it's a birthday, a wedding, or a festival, our caterers have you covered."
- After: "We cater birthdays and weddings." (Keep only the cases the user actually mentioned.)

### 20. Colon punchlines

Why it reads as AI: a short setup, a colon, then the "reveal". "The result: happier customers." "The best part: it's free." Once is fine. Line after line of it reads like a template.

- Before: "We changed the timetable. The result: fewer late buses."
- After: "Since we changed the timetable, fewer buses run late."

### 21. Brackets on every sentence

Why it reads as AI: nearly every sentence carries an aside in brackets, as if the writer can't commit to a main point. "(which is important)", "(and that's okay)", "(more on that below)".

- Before: "The meeting (scheduled for Friday) will cover the budget (which has changed)."
- After: "Friday's meeting will cover the new budget."

## Openings and endings

### 22. Throat-clearing openers

Why it reads as AI: the first line warms up instead of starting. "In today's fast-paced world", "In the ever-evolving world of", "Let's dive in", "Whether you're a student or a professional", "Have you ever wondered".

- Before: "In today's fast-paced world, staying organized is more important than ever."
- After: start with the actual point: "Here's how I keep track of bills."

### 23. Restating the request

Why it reads as AI: "Certainly! Here is a professional email about your leave request." That line belongs to a chatbot, not to the email.

- Fix: remove it. The text should begin where the real text begins.

### 24. Summary closers

Why it reads as AI: the last paragraph repeats the whole thing. "In conclusion", "Overall", "To sum up", "In summary", "Ultimately".

- Before: "In conclusion, the new policy offers many benefits for all employees."
- After: cut it, or end on the last real piece of information.

### 25. One-line dramatic closers

Why it reads as AI: a short punchy line at the end, alone in its own paragraph. "And that changes everything." "The future is here." "Let that sink in."

- Before: "We moved the team to a four day week. Productivity went up. Let that sink in."
- After: "We moved the team to a four day week, and productivity went up."

### 26. Chatbot sign-offs

Why it reads as AI: "I hope this helps!", "Let me know if you have any other questions!", "Feel free to reach out!", "Happy writing!" at the end of a letter or post where they make no sense.

- Fix: remove, or replace with the sign-off a person would use in that setting ("Thanks, Arjun").

### 27. Circling back openers

Why it reads as AI: stock follow-up lines used as a first line, even when there was nothing to circle back to. "Just wanted to circle back", "Touching base on", "Following up on my previous email" when there was no previous email, "Hope you had a great weekend!" on a Thursday.

- Before: "Just wanted to circle back and touch base regarding the invoice."
- After: "Have you had a chance to look at the invoice from 4 June?" (Use the real date only if the user gave it.)

### 28. Engagement bait endings

Why it reads as AI: social posts that end with a question the writer doesn't really want answered, or a call to comment. "What do you think? Let me know in the comments!", "Agree?", "Drop a 🙌 if this resonates", "Tag someone who needs this".

- Before: "I finally finished my first half marathon. What's your fitness goal this year? Let me know below! 👇"
- After: "I finally finished my first half marathon. My legs are still recovering."

Keep a question only if the user really wants replies, like asking friends for restaurant suggestions.

## Formatting

### 29. Em dashes everywhere

Why it reads as AI: chatbots use the long em dash constantly, often two or three per paragraph, for asides that a person would write with a comma or brackets. Also watch for the en dash used the same way.

- Before: "The meeting (em dash) which was supposed to be short (em dash) ran for two hours."
- After: "The meeting was supposed to be short. It ran for two hours."

Replace with a comma, colon, full stop, or brackets. Keep a dash only if the user clearly uses them in their own writing and it is a single one.

### 30. Bold labels on every bullet

Why it reads as AI: lists where every item starts with a bold phrase and a colon. "**Flexibility:** You can work from home."

- Before: "**Location:** Near the metro. **Price:** Affordable. **Space:** Spacious."
- After: "It's near the metro, the rent is fair, and the rooms are big."

### 31. Headings and bullets on short text

Why it reads as AI: a three paragraph email broken into headed sections and bullet lists. People write short things as short paragraphs.

- Fix: for emails, messages, and posts under about 200 words, use plain paragraphs unless the user's original had a list.

### 32. Emoji as decoration

Why it reads as AI: an emoji at the start of every line or heading (rocket, sparkles, light bulb, check mark). Real people use emoji, but not as bullet points.

- Fix: remove emoji the user did not use. Keep ones they did use, in the places they used them.

### 33. Title Case In Every Heading

Why it reads as AI: "How To Write A Better Email" when the rest of the document uses normal sentence case.

- Fix: match the user's own style. Default to sentence case.

### 34. Curly quotes and odd characters in plain text

Why it reads as AI: curly quotes, non-breaking spaces, or a stray ellipsis character where the user types straight quotes and three dots. Small, but visible in chat apps and plain text forms.

- Fix: match the characters the user already uses.

## Tone

### 35. Overly eager friendliness

Why it reads as AI: "Great question!", "Absolutely!", "I'd be delighted to", "What a wonderful idea!" in a message to a landlord.

- Before: "Absolutely! I'd be more than happy to help you with that!"
- After: "Sure, I can do that."

### 36. Fake empathy

Why it reads as AI: "I completely understand how frustrating this must be for you" as a stock opener, before getting to the point. Real empathy is specific.

- Before: "I understand how frustrating this situation must be for you."
- After: "Sorry the parcel came two days late, especially with your trip on Friday."

### 37. Positivity at all costs

Why it reads as AI: every problem is "an opportunity", every setback "a learning experience", and endings are always upbeat. Wrong in complaints, apologies, and especially condolences.

- Before: "While the delay was unfortunate, it gave us a valuable chance to grow."
- After: "The delay cost us two weeks. Here's what we're changing."

### 38. Generic examples

Why it reads as AI: "for example, a business might want to improve its efficiency". No real detail. People give specific examples from their own life.

- Fix: use the user's real detail if they gave one. If not, ask, or leave the example out. Do not invent one and present it as theirs.

### 39. Talking down

Why it reads as AI: explaining the obvious, or telling the reader how to feel. "This is a great reminder that communication is key."

- Fix: cut the moral. Trust the reader.

## Everyday writing

These are clichés tied to particular kinds of writing. The matching writing module has more detail. Resume and business buzzwords live in `lists/buzzwords.md`, so they are not repeated here.

### 40. Template letter phrases

Why it reads as AI: phrases that sound copied from a sample letter. "I am writing this letter to bring to your kind notice that", "Kindly do the needful", "I would like to humbly request", "Thanking you in anticipation".

- Before: "I am writing this letter to bring to your kind notice that the street light near Gate 2 is not working. Kindly do the needful."
- After: "The street light near Gate 2 has not worked since 10 March. Please arrange a repair."

Some Indian formal letters still expect a few of these formulas, such as "Respected Sir" or "Yours faithfully" (see `writing/formal-letters.md`). Keep the ones the setting calls for. Flag them only when they pile up, or when they turn up in a casual message.

### 41. Biodata clichés

Why it reads as AI: matrimonial profiles that use the same stock words as thousands of others. "God-fearing", "homely", "well-settled", "from a respectable family", "simple living, high thinking". They say little about the person.

- Before: "She is a homely, god-fearing girl from a well-settled, respectable family."
- After: "Priya is a chemistry teacher in Pune. She loves cooking for friends and goes trekking most weekends." (Only details the user gave.)

Keep the words the user or their family clearly want to keep. Always flag "fair", "fair complexion", and similar skin-tone words, and gently suggest dropping them. Never add them.

### 42. Listing clichés

Why it reads as AI: product listings that could describe anything. "Premium quality", "must-have", "perfect for every occasion", "elevate your style", "best in class", "you won't regret it".

- Before: "Premium quality kurti, a must-have for every wardrobe. Perfect for every occasion!"
- After: "Cotton kurti, sizes S to XL. Light enough for summer office wear. Colour may look slightly darker than in the photo." (Facts only from the seller.)

## How to use this list

- In rewrite and draft mode, fix what you find without lecturing. The user sees only the change summary.
- In check mode, group findings in plain words: "Lots of long dashes", "The ending repeats the start", "A few words that chatbots overuse (delve, crucial)". Quote short bits as evidence.
- Do not strip out every comma, list, or formal word in the name of sounding human. Over-correcting is its own tell: forced slang, fake typos, and random short sentences read as fake too.
- Patterns 40 to 42 apply only to their kind of writing. A "Respected Sir" in a bank letter is normal, the same line in a WhatsApp message is not.
- If the user's own writing has one of these habits and they clearly like it (they use "Furthermore" in every email they've shown you), leave it.
